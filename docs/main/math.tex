\section{Mathematical relationships}\label{math}
We now turn to deriving the mathematical relationship between:
the distribution of true/total durations in sex work
among the source population: $x \sim f(x)$, and
the distribution of observed/censored durations in sex work
among participants in a cross-sectional survey: $z \sim f(z|s)$,
given survey participation status $s$.
We also consider two survey sampling cases:
an ideal case which is representative of the active population, and
a more plausible case which undersamples individuals in the early years of sex work.
For reference throughout this section,
Table~\ref{tab:notation} summarizes our notation, while
Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr.1} illustrates
example distributions for $f(x)$, $f(z|s)$, and intermediates thereof.

\paragraph{Length-biased sampling}%
The probability of an individual being active at any given time
is proportional to their total duration $x$;
a phenomenon well-described (\eg in cancer epidemiology and renewal theory)
as "length-biased sampling" or the "renewal paradox" \cite{Kafadar2009,Ross2003}.
Thus, the distribution of total durations among the active population is
\cite{Ross2003}:
% LW: is the detailed derivation in this REF?
%     if so - I would make that clear or show in Appendix if not already in REF.
% JK: there is not really anything to derive I would say -
%     just multiply the two proportional terms together and normalize
\begin{equation}
  f(x|a) = \norm ~ x ~ f(x)
  \label{eq:f(x|a)}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is a normalizing constant.
These total durations among the active population are typically unobserved,
except in cohort studies which follow participants until they exit sex work.
From \eqref{eq:f(x|a)} we can see that
the mean total duration among the active population $\E[x|a]$
is always larger than the mean among the source population $\E[x]$
(Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr}, green vs black circles);
specifically, we have that \cite{Zelen1969}:
\begin{equation}
  \E[x|a] = \E[x] \left(1 + \CV^2[x]\right)
  \label{eq:E[x|a]}
\end{equation}
% JK: Interestingly, this is the same equation for "effective contact rate"
%     based on mean/variance in contact rates in a heterogeneous population
%     for computing R0 - see Eq. (0.4) in Boily1997
This is because greater variability in durations among the source population,
as measured by the coefficient of variation: $\CV[x]$,
allows those with longer durations to increasingly dominate the active population.
In reality, aging naturally constrains maximum durations,
which in turn constrains the variance,
such that typically $\CV[x] \le 2$.
We also note that the probability of observing
active individuals with very short total durations is inherently very small
(Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr.1}, green solid line).

\paragraph{Censoring}
If survey timing is effectively random, then
observed durations at the time of survey ($z$) will be right-censored
and uniformly distributed between 0 and the total duration ($x$):
\begin{equation}
  f(z|x) = \txn{Unif}(0,x) =
  \begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{x} & \txn{if}~ 0 \le z \le x \\
    0 & \txn{else}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:f(z|x)}
\end{equation}
Since the mean of $\txn{Unif}(0,x)$ is $\frac{x}{2}$,
the mean censored duration is always half the mean total duration
among the active population: $\E[z|a] = \frac{1}{2} \E[x|a]$
(Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr.1}, green crosses vs circles).
This idea appears to be well-known, and likely motivates
doubling the mean $\E[z|a]$ to estimate $\E[x]$ in method~3 of \cite{Fazito2012}
--- \ie to "undo" halving the mean due to censoring.
However, method~3 does not account for length-biased sampling noted above,
and thus actually estimates the mean total duration among the active population $\E[x|a]$,
which we know to be greater than among the source population $\E[x]$ per \eqref{eq:E[x|a]}.%
\footnote{\citet{Fazito2012} partially acknowledge this issue
  by opting to use the median over the mean of $z|a$ where possible,
  since the median is less sensitive to outliers.
  However, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:toy.est},
  doubling the median $\M[z|a]$ still overestimates $\E[x]$,
  just to a lesser degree than doubling the mean $\E[z|a]$,
  since the median will always be less than the mean for right-skewed distributions.}
\par
If total durations among the source population are exponentially distributed:
$f(x) \sim \txn{Exp}(\lambda)$, then $\CV[x] = 1$, and so
the mean \emph{censored} duration among the \emph{active} population will equal
the mean \emph{total} duration among the \emph{source} population (without doubling):
$\E[z|a] = \E[x]$ if $f(x) \sim \txn{Exp}(\lambda)$.
In other words, under these conditions,
the mean reported duration in a typical cross-sectional survey

will be an unbiased estimator of the target mean duration $\E[x]$.
Moreover, even the \emph{distributions} will be identical:
$f(z|a) = f(x) = \txn{Exp}(\lambda)$, as we will show below.
This is particularly notable since
compartmental models often implicitly assume exponentially distributed durations.

from the distribution of total durations among the source population $f(x)$
--- \eqref{eq:f(z|a).full} gives a complete derivation \cite{Tufto2017}:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    f(z|a) &= \int_0^\infty f(z|x) ~ f(x|a) ~ dx \\
           &= \norm ~ \left(1 - F_x(z)\right)
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:f(z|a)}
\end{equation}
As it turns out, this is simply
the "tail distribution" or "survival function" of total durations $x$
(one minus the cumulative distribution function),
normalized to have unit area under the curve (via $K$).
For the exponential case, we have:
$F_x = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, $K = \lambda^{-1}$, and thus
$f(z|a) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x} = f(x)$.
\par
In general, $\CV[z|a]$ is typically drawn towards 1 relative to $\CV[x]$,
attenuating skewness when $\CV[x] > 1$, and
amplifying skewness when $\CV[x] < 1$
(Figure \ref{fig:toy.distr.1}, green dashed vs black solid lines).
However, we believe that
$\CV[x] > 1$ is more likely than $\CV[x] < 1$ for two reasons.
First, heavy-tailed distributions having $\CV[x] > 1$
arise naturally via selection effects
in populations with heterogeneous rates \cite{Balan2020}.
Second, most parametric distributions with $\CV[x] < 1$
have $f(x\sp\rarr0)\sp\rarr0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr}),
which effectively implies
that few people exit sex work shortly after starting, or
that most people stay in sex work for a "minimum" period,
which seems unrealistic.
If $\CV[x] > 1$ is indeed more likely, then
the skewness of the observable duration distribution $f(z|a)$
will generally underestimate
the skewness of the true duration distribution $f(x)$.

\paragraph{Further sampling bias}
If the survey further undersamples individuals in the early years of sex work
--- \ie beyond effects of length-biased sampling,
due to delays in self-identification as a sex worker ---
this probability of being sampled will be a function of
current observed/censored duration ($z$) not true/total duration ($x$),
so we denote it $f(s|z)$.
Many parameterizations are possible; \eg \eqref{eq:f(s|z)}.
The distribution of observed durations among survey participants
is then updated from \eqref{eq:f(z|a)} simply as:
\begin{equation}
  f(z|s) = \norm ~ f(s|z) \left(1 - F_x(z)\right)
  \label{eq:f(z|s)}
\end{equation}

the three methods considered by \citet{Fazito2012}

We see that at low $\CV[x]$, nearly all methods converge on the true $\E[x]$.
However, as $\CV[x]$ increases,
length-biased sampling causes stronger selection towards
longer total durations among the active population,
raising the corresponding estimates of $\sE[x]$ according to \eqref{eq:E[x|a]},
\eg twice as large at $\CV[x] = 1$.
Median censored durations exhibit a similar trend,
but lag below means (true for all right-skewed distributions)
to a degree which depends on the family,
making such medians less useful estimators of $\E[x]$.
For all method~3 estimates, undersampling of early sex work
(here assuming $\pp = 0.5, \tt = 1$ year)
predictably biases estimates slightly higher;
this bias is also larger for smaller $\E[x]$ (not shown).
Finally, turnover-based estimates (method~2) tends to be most consistent,
only slightly overestimating $\E[x]$ as $\CV[x]$ increases;
however, this method is especially sensitive to
undersampling of early sex work, which biases estimates upwards.
\par
Nevertheless, the best simple estimate of $\E[x]$ is likely $\E[z|s]$,
assuming $\CV[x] \approx 1$, or more generally:

\begin{equation}
  \sE[x] = \frac{1}{2} ~ \E[z|s] \left(1 + \CV^2[x]\right)
\end{equation}
