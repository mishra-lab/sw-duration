\paragraph{Length-biased sampling}%
The probability of an individual being active at any given time
is proportional to their total duration $x$;
a phenomenon well-described (\eg in cancer epidemiology and renewal theory)
as "length-biased sampling" or the "renewal paradox" \cite{Kafadar2009,Ross2003}.
Thus, the distribution of total durations among the active population is
\cite{Ross2003,Tufto2017}:
% LW: is the detailed derivation in this REF?
%     if so - I would make that clear or show in Appendix if not already in REF.
% JK: there is not really anything to derive I would say -
%     just multiply the two proportional terms together and normalize
\begin{equation}
  f(x|a) = \frac{1}{K} ~ x ~ f(x)
  \label{eq:f(x|a)}
\end{equation}
% JK: I use "K" here rather than E[x]
%     to simplify / highlight the proportionality with x & f(x)
%     but I've gone back and forth about just using E[x] in the equation(s) directly
where $K$ is a normalizing constant equal to
the mean total duration among the source population $\E[x]$.
These total durations among the active population $x|a$ are typically unobserved,
except in cohort studies which follow participants until they exit sex work.
From \eqref{eq:f(x|a)} we can show that
the mean total duration among the active population $\E[x|a]$
is always larger than the mean among the source population $\E[x]$
(Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr}, green vs black circles);
specifically, we have \cite{Zelen1969}:
\begin{equation}
  \E[x|a] = \E[x] (1 + \CV^2[x])
  \label{eq:E[x|a]}
\end{equation}
% JK: Interestingly, this is the same equation for "effective contact rate"
%     based on mean/variance in contact rates in a heterogeneous population
%     for computing R0 - see Eq. (0.4) in Boily1997
This is because greater variability in durations among the source population,
as measured by the coefficient of variation: $\CV[x]$,
allows those with longer durations to increasingly dominate the active population.
For reference, we expect that $\CV[x] \in (1,2)$ in this context, for three reasons.

First, heavy-tailed distributions having $\CV[x] > 1$ can arise via selection effects
in a population with heterogeneous exit rates \cite{Balan2020}.
Second, most parametric distributions with $\CV[x] < 1$
have $f(x\sp\rarr0)\sp\rarr0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr}), which effectively implies
that few people exit sex work shortly after starting, or
that most people stay in sex work for a "minimum" period,
which seems unrealistic.
Finally, aging naturally constrains maximum durations via a competing risk of death,
which in turn constrains duration variance,
such that usually $\CV[x] < 2$.
\par
Returning to \eqref{eq:f(x|a)},
we also note that the probability of observing
active individuals with very short total durations is inherently very small
(Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr.1}, green solid line).
A related insight is that the \emph{inactive} population (denoted $\bar{a}$),
includes many future or former sex workers with short total durations
(Figure~\ref{fig:diag}, gray),
and thus has smaller mean total duration than the source or active populations:
$\E[x|\bar{a}] < \E[x] < \E[x|a]$.

\paragraph{Censoring}
Next we consider a hypothetical survey among the active population.
If timing of the survey is effectively random, then
observed durations at survey time ($z$) will be right-censored
and uniformly distributed between 0 and the total duration ($x$):
\begin{equation}
  f(z|x) = \txn{Unif}(0,x) =
  \begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{x} & \txn{if}~ 0 \le z \le x \\
    0 & \txn{else}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:f(z|x)}
\end{equation}
Since the mean of $\txn{Unif}(0,x)$ is $\frac{x}{2}$,
the mean censored duration will be half
the mean total duration among the active population
(Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr.1}, green crosses vs circles):
\begin{equation}
  \E[z|a] = \frac{1}{2} \E[x|a]
  \label{eq:E[z|a]}
\end{equation}
This idea appears to be well-known, and likely motivates
doubling the mean $\E[z|a]$ to estimate $\E[x]$ in method~3 of \cite{Fazito2012}
--- \ie to "undo" halving the mean due to censoring.
However, method~3 does not account for length-biased sampling,
and thus actually estimates the mean total duration among the active population $\E[x|a]$,
which we know to be greater than among the source population $\E[x]$ per \eqref{eq:E[x|a]}.%
\footnote{\citet{Fazito2012} partially acknowledge this issue
  by opting to use the median over the mean of $z|a$ where possible,
  since the median is less sensitive to outliers.
  However, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:toy.est},
  doubling the median $\M[z|a]$ still overestimates $\E[x]$,
  just to a lesser degree than doubling the mean $\E[z|a]$,
  since the median will always be less than the mean for right-skewed distributions.}

\paragraph{Complete distribution}
To consider both length-biased sampling and censoring simultaneously,
we can combine \eqrefa{eq:E[x|a]}{eq:E[z|a]} to relate
the mean total duration among the source population $\E[x]$ to
the mean censored duration among the active population $\E[z|s]$:
\begin{equation}
  \E[x] = \frac{2}{1 + \CV^2[x]} ~ \E[z|s]
  \label{eq:E[x].cv}
\end{equation}
However, this is not immediately useful unless $\CV[x]$ is known.
Alternatively, by combining \eqrefa{eq:f(x|a)}{eq:f(z|x)}, we can relate
the complete distribution of total durations among the source population $f(x)$ to
the distribution of censored durations among the active population $f(z|a)$
--- \eqref{eq:f(z|a).full} gives a complete derivation \cite{Tufto2017}:

\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    f(z|a) &= \int_0^\infty f(z|x) ~ f(x|a) ~ dx \\
           &= \frac{1 - F_x(z)}{\E[x]}
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:f(z|a)}
\end{equation}
As it turns out, this is simply
the "survival function" or "tail distribution" of total durations $x$
(one minus the cumulative distribution function $F_x$),
normalized to have unit area under the curve via $\E[x]$ as in \eqref{eq:f(x|a)}.
Since this cumulative distribution is initially $F_x(z=0) = 0$ by definition,
we can estimate $\E[x]$ as
the inverse of the proportion of active sex workers who "just started"
(Figure~\ref{fig:toy.distr.1}, y-intercept of green dashed lines):%
\footnote{\eqref{eq:E[x].z0} is essentially
  a more precise definition of method~2 in \cite{Fazito2012}.}
\begin{equation}
  \E[x] = \frac{1}{f(z=0|a)}
  \label{eq:E[x].z0}
\end{equation}
We can then recover $f(x)$ by differentiating $f(z|a)$ and multiplying by $\E[x]$:
\begin{equation}
  f(x) = - ~ \E[x] \frac{d}{dz} f(z|a)
  \label{eq:f(x)}
\end{equation}
\eqref{eq:f(x)} is only useful if the complete distribution $f(z|a)$ is known,
or can be approximated \eg via histogram.
If only summary statistics for $f(z|a)$ are available,
we can still leverage \eqref{eq:f(z|a)} to infer $f(x)$, as we show in \sref{app}.

\paragraph{Exponential case}
A special case arises if durations are exponentially distributed:
$f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$,
which is often implicitly assumed in compartmental simulation models \cite{Garnett2011}.
Since $\E[x] = \lambda^{-1}$ and $F_x = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$,
we find that: $f(z|a) = f(x)$.
That is, the distributions of
\emph{total} durations among the \emph{source} population and
\emph{censored} durations among the \emph{active} population
will be exactly equal.
Under these conditions,
the mean reported duration in a typical cross-sectional survey $\E[z|a]$
will directly estimate the target mean duration $\E[x]$.
This equality is also indicated by \eqref{eq:E[x].cv},
since $\CV[x] = 1$ for the exponential distribution.
However, as noted above, often $\CV[x] > 1$,
in which case $\E[z|a]$ would overestimate $\E[x]$.

\paragraph{Further sampling bias}
Until now we have assumed that surveys reach
a representative sample of the active population.
If a given survey further undersamples individuals in the early years of sex work
--- \ie beyond effects of length-biased sampling,
due to delays in self-identification as a sex worker \cite{Cheuk2020} ---
this probability of being sampled will be a function of
current observed/censored duration ($z$) not true/total duration ($x$),
so we denote it $f(s|z)$.
Many parameterizations of $f(s|z)$ are possible; \eg \eqref{eq:f(s|z)} below.
To incorporate this further sampling bias, we update
the distribution of observed durations among survey participants
from \eqref{eq:f(z|a)} as:
\begin{equation}
  f(z|s) = f(s|z) ~ \frac{1 - F_x(z)}{\E[x]}
  \label{eq:f(z|s)}
\end{equation}
In this context, $f(x)$ cannot be directly determined via \eqref{eq:f(x)},
but it can be inferred as we show in \sref{app}.

\paragraph{Revisiting prior methods to estimate $\E[x]$}
% JK: this section now feels slightly disconnected,

We conclude this section by revisiting
the three methods applied by \citet{Fazito2012}
to estimate the "true" mean duration in sex work $\E[x]$ (Table~\ref{tab:meth}).
Figure~\ref{fig:toy.est} plots:
the typically unobserved mean and median of total durations (method~1),%
\footnote{We are being generous to method~1 here by assuming
  former sex workers are representative of the \emph{source} population,
  whereas only the \emph{inactive} population is sampled in practice.}
the reciprocal proportion of censored durations less than 1 year (method~2),
and \emph{double} the means and medians of censored durations (method~3),
across a range of $\CV[x]$ and distribution families for $f(x)$ with true $\E[x] = 5$.
We see that at low $\CV[x]$, nearly all methods converge on the true $\E[x]$.
However, as $\CV[x]$ increases,
length-biased sampling causes stronger selection towards
longer total durations among the active population,
raising the corresponding estimates of $\E[x]$ according to \eqref{eq:E[x|a]},
\eg twice as large at $\CV[x] = 1$.
Median censored durations exhibit a similar trend,
but lag below means (true for all right-skewed distributions)
to a degree which depends on the family,
making such medians less useful estimators of $\E[x]$ if the family is unknown.
For all method~3 estimates, undersampling of early sex work
(here assuming $\pp = 0.5, \tt = 1$ year)
predictably biases estimates slightly higher;
this bias is also larger for smaller $\E[x]$ (not shown).
Finally, turnover-based estimates (method~2) tends to be most consistent,
only slightly overestimating $\E[x]$ as $\CV[x]$ increases.
However, this method is especially sensitive to
undersampling of early sex work, which biases estimates upwards.
