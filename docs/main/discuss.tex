\par % implications for HIV incidence
Our estimates of mean duration in sex work were
significantly less than those reported by \citet{Fazito2012}.
This was expected, as we showed how both methods 2 and 3 can overestimate mean duration,
especially if surveys undersample individuals in early sex work.
Shorter durations means that that HIV incidence in sex work

could be higher than previously estimated via transmission models,
since there is less time for infections to accumulate
to match observed HIV prevalence in sex work \cite{Knight2020,Anderegg2024}.

among female sex workers in Sub-Saharan Africa

this might be partly explained by recruitment of

relatively higher-risk wider population women

\par % implications for prioritization
Shorter durations, and thus more rapid sex work turnover, could also suggest that
a greater proportion of women in the wider population might have previously sold sex.

Indeed, quasi-anonymous polling booth surveys have indicated
substantial under-reporting lifetime sex work engagement for both women and men
in face-to-face interviews \cite{Lowndes2012,Behanzin2013}.
A greater proportion of prevalent infections in the population overall
might then be attributable to brief, unreported periods of sex work,
additionally considering unique vulnerabilities of early sex work \cite{Cheuk2020}.
We reiterate that such brief periods in sex work are
especially difficult to observe in cross-sectional studies
due to length-biased sampling \cite{Ross2003}.
\par % implications for modelling
We also inferred that distributions of total durations in sex work overall
are likely "heavy-tailed", such that
rates of exit decline with longer duration in sex work.
This declining exit rate could reflect
selection effects acting on fixed between-person heterogeneity in
a propensity to remain in sex work \cite{Balan2020},
and/or within-person changes that make it easier to remain in sex work,

including self-identification as a sex worker, and
changing social ties with family, friends, and clients,
\cite{Neufeld2023,Bhattacharjee2023,Ingabire2012,Bowen2011,Lora2025}.
While individual-based simulation models could specify
such heterogeneity or declining exit rates directly,
compartmental simulation models would need
two or more sex work states to reproduce this pattern --- \eg

separate short and long duration sex work trajectories, and/or
separate early and late sex work periods.
Indeed, such states would also be useful to capture
unique risks associated with early sex work
\cite{Neufeld2023,Bhattacharjee2023}.

We also note that a cohort design
does not necessarily simplify estimation of "true" sex work durations,
as sampling can still be length-biased
(depending on how participants are recruited)
and significant loss-to-follow-up can occur \cite{Ward2006}.
While the proposed Bayesian model can leverage
multiple kinds of aggregate data on durations,
future cross-sectional studies could most helpfully report
complete duration histograms, where possible,
and the following key summary statistics:
minimum, maximum, mean, median, and interquartile range of durations,
as well as the proportion of active individuals in sex work 1 year or less.

\paragraph{Limitations}
Our work has several limitations.
First, we did not model any gaps (temporary breaks) in sex work.
Many estimates of sex work duration are derived from
the difference between respondents' current age and the age they report first selling sex,
which implicitly ignores such gaps.
Respondents may also report "duration in sex work" in this way when asked directly,
especially since unsuccessful attempts to exit sex work can be stigmatized \cite{Lora2025}.

Second, we did not consider any recall/reporting biases,
as entry into sex work is likely a memorable life-course event
\cite{Sandy2025,Cimino2017,Ingabire2012,Bowen2011,Lora2025},
and self-identified active sex workers do not, to our knowledge, have
obvious motivations for under-reporting durations.
% SM: nice
We additionally assumed that sex work turnover is stable over time,
despite empirical evidence of changing sex work dynamics in several contexts
\cite{Anderegg2024,Anderson2026}.
Finally, our re-analysis of duration data from \cite{Fazito2012}
did not include more recent or individual-level data,
since our motivation was mainly to compare
results using the Bayesian model versus prior estimation methods.
Additionally, in forthcoming work we will synthesize
individual-level data from 183 studies \cite{Anderson2026}.

\paragraph{Future work}
Our Bayesian model could be extended in several ways.
First, it might be more useful to model sex work durations
via semi/parametric hazard rates of exiting sex work,
rather than parametric duration distributions.
A hazard rate approach could allow and incorporation of
more intuitive parameter priors (\eg 0.5--50\% exit rate per month),
covariates (\eg under proportional hazards assumptions) \cite{Kalbfleisch2023}, and
frailties (\eg to capture unmodelled heterogeneity) \cite{Balan2020}.
Relatedly, joint modelling of duration and age and/or calendar time
could help characterize relevant trends noted above
\eg for HIV/STI transmission modelling \cite{Anderegg2024,Anderson2026}.
Such extensions might be supported by related work estimating
durations (also called sojourn times) in chronic disease states
for modelling and analysis of screening programs \cite{Zelen1969}.

\paragraph{Conclusion}
The distribution of durations in sex work is
a key determinant of HIV/STI dynamics and
an essential input to HIV/STI transmission models.
Such durations are commonly reported in cross-sectional studies,
where they are subject to two competing biases:
length-biased sampling (increases the mean)
and censoring (decreases the mean).
The former bias, omitted from prior work, is likely stronger, such that
the mean duration reported in cross-sectional studies
actually overestimates rather than underestimates the "true" mean duration.
Methods herein also support inference of
the complete distribution of "true" durations,
given a complete distribution of reported durations.
