\section{Preliminaries}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Assumptions}
For simplicity, we first assume that periods of sex work
can be precisely and instantaneously delineated,
\ie overlooking blurry boundaries between sex work and transactional sex,
and definitions of sex work based on activity in the past 6 months, etc.
\cite{Scorgie2012}.
We further assume that survey participants can
perfectly recall periods of sex work.
Finally, we assume that all populations are at equilibrium,
despite empiric evidence of changing sex work dynamics in several contexts
\cite{Anderegg2024}.

\paragraph{Existing estimation methods}
% JK: this may end up in the introduction
In their global scoping review of HIV risk behaviour durations,
\citet{Fazito2012} considered three methods to estimate
mean duration selling/buying sex (Table~\ref{tab:ests}):
1. mean total duration among those finished selling/buying sex;
2. reciprocal of the proportion who started selling/buying sex within the past year; and
3. double the mean/median reported duration among those actively selling/buying sex.
Notably, among the 118 estimates of duration selling or buying sex
identified by \citeauthor{Fazito2012}:
the vast majority used method~3 (86\%),
a small faction used method~2 (14\%), and
none used method~1.
Studies also commonly report
the proportions of respondents selling/buying sex
for pre-specified duration ranges,
\eg \{0--1, 1--5, 5+\} years,
though it's not clear if/how such data were used in \cite{Fazito2012}.

\paragraph{Who exactly are we talking about?}
Before continuing, it is helpful to distinguish two populations:
individuals who will ever sell/buy sex in their lifetime, and
individuals who are actively selling/buying sex.
The former "source population" is the sampling frame for method~1,
and tends to include more individuals who sell/buy sex for shorter durations;
the latter "active population" is the sampling frame for methods 2 and 3,
and tends to include more individuals who sell/buy sex for longer durations
(Figure~\ref{fig:diag.pop}).
It is tempting to think that,
since "sex work" strata in compartmental models reflect the active population,
rates of exit from sex work should be derived from this population.
However, this is not the case, because
these rates apply to all individuals \emph{entering} sex work,
which reflects the source population.%
\footnote{An analogous example:
  the mean duration of an infection should be characterized by
  a cohort of individuals with \emph{incident} infections,
  not a cross-section of individuals with \emph{prevalent} infections.}
A final population worth distinguishing is
the "sampled population" in a given cross-sectional survey,
which we assume intends to be representative of the active population,
but might undersample some individuals,
\eg those in the early years of sex work,
due to delays in self-identification as a sex worker
\cite{Mishra2012,Cheuk2020}.
