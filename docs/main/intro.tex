\section{Introduction}\label{intro}
Epidemiological studies and healthcare programs
are often interested in quantifying distributions of durations,
such as durations of transient health states or exposure to risk/protective factors.
Estimates of these distributions can help
characterize a population's cumulative exposure \cite{Ben-Shlomo2023}, and
inform inputs to state-transition simulation models \cite{Garnett2011}.

One prominent example is duration in sex work
--- selling sex for money or equivalent ---
reflecting a transient period of elevated risk for acquisition and transmission of
HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STI) due to
intersecting structural, behavioural, and biological factors
\cite{Scorgie2012,Shannon2015,Baral2015msw}.
While entry into sex work is driven primarily by economic and social instability,
exit is further mediated by aging, stable partnering, stigma, and violence
\cite{Ingabire2012,Bowen2011,Lora2025}.
Simulation models of STI, including HIV, transmission have highlighted how
unmet prevention and treatment needs within sex work
are often drivers of epidemic emergence and persistence
\cite{Boily2002,Watts2010,Silhol2026}.
In such models, rates of exit from sex work are typically defined as
the inverse of the mean duration in sex work
--- \eg a 5-year mean duration corresponding to a 20\% exit rate per year.
If the sex worker population size is approximately stable over time, then
exiting sex workers must be replaced by a similar number of entering sex workers
--- \eg corresponding to 20\% "turnover" \cite{Knight2020}.
% JK: this implicit definition of turnover is not ideal - might try to revise again later
\par
Mean durations in sex work are thus
key determinants of HIV/STI epidemiology, and key inputs to transmission models,
particularly those aiming to compare the impacts of different prioritized services.
For example, 10\% HIV prevalence among sex workers
in a context with 1-year mean duration (rapid turnover)
suggests a high HIV incidence rate, whereas 10\% HIV prevalence
in a context with 10-year mean durations (slow turnover)
suggests a lower HIV incidence rate,
since prevalence has more time to accumulate \cite{Knight2020,Anderegg2024}.%
\footnote{Assuming HIV prevalence among new sex workers is low.}
Turnover can also moderate levels of
program / service coverage among sex workers (\eg PrEP),
where rapid turnover could create a constant influx of new sex workers to reach, and
potentially high loss-to-follow-up among those exiting sex work,
whereas slower turnover could make it easier to attain higher levels of coverage.
Finally, duration in sex work can further influence onward transmission risk,
where longer durations theoretically allow
more onward transmission following acquisition (in the absence of treatment).

\citet{Fazito2012} conducted
a global scoping review and regional syntheses of mean duration in sex work,
with estimates ranging from 2.8 years in Asia to 12 years in Latin America.
This review focused on cross-sectionally reported durations,
and applied three methods to estimate mean duration, as data permitted:
1. mean duration among former sex workers;
2. inverse of the proportion who started sex work within the past year among active sex workers; and
3. double the mean/median duration among active sex workers, to account for right-censoring.
Yet, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:meth},
each of these methods has challenges/limitations --- mainly related to:
difficulty sampling the target population (methods 1,2) \cite{Ward2006}, and
overlooking length-biased sampling (methods 2,3) \cite{Kafadar2009},
as we will discuss further herein.
Only some of these challenges/limitations were acknowledged in \cite{Fazito2012}.
Notably, some studies also report the proportions of respondents
selling sex for pre-specified duration ranges, \eg \mbox{\{0--2, 2--5, 5+\}} years,
but it's not clear if/how such data were used in \cite{Fazito2012}.

Considering these challenges/limitations,
we posit that estimates of mean duration in sex work
reported in \cite{Fazito2012}, and elsewhere using similar methods,
could be substantially biased.
Indeed, there appears to be uncertainty in general regarding
how durations reported in cross-sectional studies
relate to true overall durations in sex work.
As one recent HIV modelling study noted
when specifying a duration parameter's prior distribution upper/lower bounds:
\emph{"We multiply the lower bound by 0.5 and upper bound by 2
  because of uncertainty in how overall duration of sex work
  relates to current duration of sex work"} \cite{Stone2021}.

\paragraph{Objectives}
To address this uncertainty
and challenges/limitations of existing methods to estimate mean duration in sex work,
we sought to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \label{obj:1}
        Derive the mathematical relationship between
        the distribution of observed durations in sex work sex from cross-sectional studies and
        the distribution of true durations in sex work overall.

        Develop and apply a Bayesian model of this relationship with aggregate data from

The remainder of our paper is structured as follows:
In \sref{pre}, we clarify some key concepts and assumptions for the work.
In \sref{math}, we derive the mathematical relationship for Objective~\ref{obj:1}.
In \sref{app}, we develop and apply the Bayesian model for Objective~\ref{obj:2}.
In \sref{disc}, we conclude with discussion of implications and future work.
