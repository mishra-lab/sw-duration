Average lifetime durations selling or buying sex
are key determinants of HIV/STI transmission dynamics and service coverage.
Such durations are usually estimated from
cross-sectional surveys of people actively selling/buying sex,
which are cheaper/simpler than longitudinal designs.
However, two competing biases must be considered for this estimation:
first, cross-sectional surveys over-sample individuals who sell/buy sex for longer;
second, essentially all survey-reported durations will be right-censored.
Prior works have not consistently or precisely addressed both of these biases.
In this paper, we illustrate exactly how
the distribution of durations reported in cross-sectional surveys
is related to the true distribution of durations, at equilibrium.
Notably, these distributions will be identical
if true durations are exponentially distributed;
otherwise, reported durations will tend to
underestimate the variance / "heavy tail" of true durations.
We then develop a parametric Bayesian model of this relationship,
and apply it to aggregate data from two previously published studies,
including a global systematic review.
Our model-estimated average durations are
significantly shorter than those previously published for all regions,

including 3.7 (\CI: 3.4--3.9) versus 5.5 years in Africa,
and 1.4 (1.2--1.5) versus 2.8--4.0 years in Asia.
These shorter average durations then suggest that
HIV incidence in sex work could be higher than previously thought,
in order to match the same HIV prevalence data.
Our approach can be applied beyond sex work,
to characterize duration distributions for any transient risk/disease state
relevant to compartmental modelling or epidemiological study.
